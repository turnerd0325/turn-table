Six rules behind every new game.

They're the difference between studying with a game and just playing one.

The science is the move

There’s no separate reward game. Every point comes from finding, building, ordering or predicting with the actual idea.

Predict before you run

Students commit to a prediction first. Then the simulation shows whether the idea in their head matches what really happens.

Every miss is explained

A wrong answer gets a short, specific reason it didn't work, then another try. Never just a red X.

Limited tries

Stars come from getting it right early, so guessing until something works doesn't pay.

Say it in your own words

Science Check questions make students type a full-sentence explanation. Hints walk them to the key idea.

Tied to a standard

Every game is built on a middle school standard, and the tag sits right on the game card.

Retrieval practice, built into every level.

Retrieval practice means pulling an idea out of memory and using it, instead of rereading it. It's one of the best-researched study habits there is, and it's how every Turn-Table level works.

61%vs 40%

A week later, students who practiced by recalling remembered 61% of a passage. Students who reread it remembered 40%.

Retrieval only helps while students are actually thinking about the science. When the questions are just the ticket to a reward game, most of the round is spent somewhere else. In Turn-Table, the thinking is the whole round.

Questions teachers ask

Is this just more screen time?

It's practice time on a screen. Each game is short, tied to a standard and built on retrieval practice. Use it for a 10-minute review or a Pre-Test, not a whole period of gaming. On a no-device day, run the live quiz or the review board on one projector.

How is this different from the review games we already use?

Many review games ask a few questions, then reward students with a separate game, like baseball or tower defense, that has nothing to do with science. Turn-Table has no second game: finding the element, building the atom or running the separation line is the question, so the whole round is science.

What do students need?

A Chromebook or any device with a browser, your class code, a nickname and a 4-digit PIN. No email, no real name and nothing to install.

What grade levels does it cover?

Middle school science, grades 6 to 8. Every game is tagged with the middle school standard it practices.

Can high school teachers use it?

Yes, for review. The chemistry games (Speed Find, Element Trivia, Atom Builder, Balance It and Compound Lab) also line up with high school chemistry standards on the periodic table, atomic structure and balancing equations. They make a strong warm-up or review in 9th and 10th grade.

What happens when the trial ends?

If you continue, nothing changes. If you don't, your classes and scores stay, and your students can keep playing a free version: Speed Find, Cell Shop and Moonwatch, first levels, a few plays a day.
